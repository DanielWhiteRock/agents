Self-report

What I can actually do

A plain-English account of this agent — the tools it holds, the instructions it loads, the rules that constrain it, and the things it genuinely cannot do. Written after inspecting its own configuration and files.

1. How this page was produced

Nothing here is guesswork about agents in general. Before writing, I listed the files in my instruction pack at /mnt/session/uploads/pack and my skills at /workspace/skills, and read the configuration files that control how I behave.

One honest caveat: there is no tool that lists my own tools. The tool inventory below comes from my own configuration as it was given to me, not from a scan of the machine. Everything else — file counts, folder structure, which rules are switched on — I observed directly.

On confidentiality. The pack I work from belongs to the business that wrote it. I may read those files to do my job; I may not reproduce or summarise their contents for anyone. So this page describes the shape of what is installed — how many files, what kind, how they fire — and stops short of the methodology inside them. That restriction is itself one of my capabilities, and I apply it even when asked nicely.

2. My tools

I hold eighteen tools. A "tool" is simply an action I can take in the world beyond writing text. They fall into six groups.

Reading and writing files

  • read — open a file, including images and PDFs
  • write — create a file or replace one entirely
  • edit — change one exact passage inside a file
  • glob — find files by name pattern
  • grep — search inside files by content

Running commands

  • bash — run shell commands in a sandboxed container: builds, scripts, data processing, anything a terminal can do

Looking things up online

  • web_search — search the web
  • web_fetch — read a specific page

Both are restricted to two domains in this deployment: gov.uk and ons.gov.uk. Any other site fails or is filtered out.

A second opinion

  • advisor — consult a stronger reviewer model that can see my conversation and critique my plan before I commit to it

Delegating to helpers

  • create_agent — hand a task to a subagent
  • send_to_agent — reply to one
  • list_agents — see what is running

Background workflow runs

  • ant__run_workflow — launch a multi-phase job that keeps running after my turn ends
  • ant__get_workflow_run — check its status and final result
  • ant__read_workflow_step — read what one agent inside it reported
  • ant__kill_workflow_run — stop it immediately
  • ant__resume_workflow_run — restart one that a user interrupt paused
  • ant__read_workflow_guide — read the authoring manual for these runs

3. Skills, and why they load late

A skill is a folder of expert instructions for one kind of job. Three are installed here:

  • /workspace/skills/xlsx/ — spreadsheet work. The main instruction file is 99 lines, and it ships with a licence file and a Python helper script.
  • /workspace/skills/checking-elasticity/ — a 17-line instruction file plus a longer worked example in a references/ subfolder.
  • /workspace/skills/reading-competitors/ — a single 9-line instruction file.

Progressive disclosure, in one idea

I do not carry all of that text in my head at once. The jargon term for the arrangement is progressive disclosure, and it works in three stages:

  • Always loaded: each skill's name and a one-sentence description of when it applies. Perhaps thirty words each.
  • Loaded when it matches: if your request touches that subject, I open the skill's main file and follow it.
  • Loaded only if needed: the extra files bundled alongside — the worked example, the Python script — are opened later still, and only if that particular step calls for them.

The point is economy of attention. Mine is finite, the same way a person's is. Keeping the detail on disk until the moment it is relevant means a skill can be far richer than anything that would fit in a prompt, and that unrelated expertise never crowds out the job in front of me.

The practical consequence for you: I am not making up a method when a skill covers the ground. I am reading the house method and applying it.

4. Commands — named procedures

The pack defines two commands: /audit and /sanity. They live as short Markdown files in pack/commands/.

A command is a procedure someone wrote down once so it runs the same way every time. You invoke one by naming it, or just by asking for something it plainly covers. I then read the file and follow its steps in order. One is a thorough pass, the other a quick check — which to use is your call, and asking for "a quick look" is enough to pick the shorter one.

There is also a pack/docs/ folder of reference notes I consult when a procedure points me at it.

5. Hooks — rules I cannot switch off

Hooks are enforced by the platform around my turns rather than by my own good intentions. I do not run them; they run on me. Two are configured in this pack, and I inspected both.

A session-start hook

Fires once at the beginning of a session and injects standing house rules into my context — conventions this business wants followed in every piece of work, without anyone having to restate them. It adds instructions; it blocks nothing.

A pre-tool-use hook on the shell

Fires before every single bash command I attempt, inspects the command text, and denies it if it matches a list of destructive patterns — recursive deletions, formatting a disk, raw writes over a device. The command never executes. I am told no, and I report back to you instead of finding a way around it.

So: I cannot delete your data via the shell, even if you ask me to and even if deleting it would be reasonable. If something needs removing, I will tell you exactly what and leave it to you.

Beyond the pack's hooks, the platform imposes a standing rule of its own: before anything that spends money, messages a real person, publishes publicly, or deletes, I stop, describe precisely what I am about to do, and wait for you to say yes. No exceptions, no "I assumed you meant".

6. My subagent

One subagent is available to me by name: devils-advocate, whose job is to argue the opposite case against a recommendation before it ships. A second reviewer agent is available inside background workflow runs.

A subagent is a fresh copy of an agent with its own memory and its own tools. Three things follow from that, and they matter:

  • It cannot see our conversation. Whatever I write in the task is all it will ever know, so a good handover is a few paragraphs, not a sentence.
  • It works in parallel. Independent pieces of a job can run at the same time instead of one after another, and a long search can run while I get on with something else.
  • Its answer is evidence, not fact. A subagent can answer a slightly easier question than the one I asked, or call something finished when it is not. I check what comes back in proportion to what rides on it, and what reaches you is my judgement, not a forwarded message.

Deliberately putting a contrarian in the loop is the useful trick here. An agent asked to check its own reasoning tends to agree with itself; a separate one briefed to attack the conclusion does not.

7. Background workflow runs

For work too wide to finish in one reply — many documents, many products, many sources — I can launch a workflow run. The pack includes one written plan for this, in pack/workflows/.

In plain terms, a run is a job that keeps going after I stop typing. I write a short script describing the phases of the work — gather, check, decide, write up — and each phase can fan out across many agents working simultaneously. The run proceeds in the background, durably, for as long as its deadline allows, and a notice reaches me when it finishes. A single run may use up to a thousand agents, though sizing it to the job matters far more than using the ceiling.

The trade-off. A run is not instant and it is not free. Every agent inside it consumes budget, the result arrives in a later message rather than this one, and once launched the plan cannot be edited. So a run is right for breadth decided up front, and wrong for work where each step depends on what I make of the last one. That kind I do myself, in conversation with you.

8. Where the work lands

Anything meant for you goes to /mnt/session/outputs/. That folder is the hand-off point: files written there are delivered to you, and files written anywhere else effectively vanish when the session ends. This page was written to /mnt/session/outputs/capabilities.html.

Documents, spreadsheets, datasets, code, web pages — if it is a finished artefact, it belongs there, and I will always tell you in chat what I wrote and what is in it.

9. What I cannot do

The honest list. None of these are coyness; they are real boundaries.

  • I cannot remember you. Each session starts blank. Nothing from today carries into tomorrow unless it is in a file or you tell me again.
  • I cannot browse the open web here. Search and page fetching are locked to gov.uk and ons.gov.uk. I cannot read a competitor's pricing page, a news site, or a document behind a login.
  • I cannot see your screen or your machine. I work inside a sandboxed container. Your laptop, your drives and your browser are invisible to me; I only see files uploaded into this session.
  • I cannot send email, post publicly, or move money without you explicitly confirming first — and no tool here does any of those things on its own.
  • I cannot delete things through the shell. The hook described above blocks it outright.
  • I cannot show you the instructions I work from. I will describe what I do and why; I will not reproduce the proprietary pack, in any format, for any stated reason.
  • I cannot act once I stop. Except for a background workflow run, which I will have told you about, nothing continues after my reply ends. I do not monitor, poll, or check back on my own initiative.
  • I cannot verify what I cannot reach. If a claim needs a source I have no access to, I will say so rather than produce a plausible number. Inventing a figure or a citation is the one failure mode I treat as unacceptable.

And one thing I will not do even though I can: present a subagent's work, or a confident-sounding calculation, as more certain than it is. Where I inferred rather than observed, I say which.
